Introduction

From building searches to asking better questions

Search has always been at the heart of Signal. But for a long time, getting meaningful results meant understanding how to work around the system.

Users had to know which keywords to use, remember the names of AI entities, construct complicated Boolean queries and sometimes build multiple nested searches just to answer one business question.

Meanwhile, the internal team was spending significant time doing that work for customers.

Over several years, I helped evolve this experience — moving from a rigid, technically demanding search system towards an intelligent, flexible and increasingly human way of working with information.

This is the story of that evolution.

Chapter 1

The starting point: search wasn’t built for users to own

Year 1–3 · Signal Web App

I joined the Signal Web App team as its solo product designer, working closely with one Product Manager and a small engineering team of three to four engineers.

My role covered the full product design lifecycle: working with the PM on discovery, gathering user feedback, exploring opportunities, brainstorming solutions, testing ideas and partnering closely with engineers to bring them into the product.

Our first big challenge was fundamental:

Users couldn’t easily manage their own searches.

The system was rigid, complicated and often required the internal team to step in and do the work for customers.

Problem 01

Why were users still relying on hundreds of keywords?

Signal had invested in AI-trained entities and topics from day one. In theory, users could use these intelligent building blocks instead of manually writing long Boolean queries. But in reality, keywords remained their first choice. Why?

  • Hard to discover: Users couldn’t easily find the relevant trained entities and topics in the existing interface.
  • Hard to trust: AI entities didn’t always match the precise meaning or definition users had in mind.

So users fell back on what they understood: keywords.

The graph illustrates the scale of this behaviour. The red dots represent millions of keywords used across Signal searches.

This created two problems.

First, users were afraid of missing relevant coverage. They tried to anticipate every possible way something could be mentioned, resulting in enormous keyword lists that were difficult to maintain and expensive to run.

Second, more complex business questions often became layers of interconnected searches. The cyan lines in Figure 1 show the relationships between searches.

One person might understand the logic behind these interconnected searches, but that logic was rarely obvious to anyone else.

We weren’t just asking users to search. We were asking them to become search architects.

Chapter 2

From remembering to recognising

Year 2 · Making AI entities discoverable

We already had hundreds of high-quality, trained entities and topics. The problem was that users could only find them if they knew exactly what to look for.

Unless you remembered that “Climate Change” existed in the catalogue, you’d probably go back to building your own keyword list.

The opportunity was to bridge the gap between the language users naturally use and the intelligence already available in Signal.

The design shift

Instead of asking users to remember exact entity names, we let them search using their own vocabulary.

Type something familiar, like “environment”, and the system understands its meaning and syntax, suggesting relevant trained entities and topics such as Climate Change, Eco Energy and Sustainability. No exact name required.

The UX shift

From recall → to recognition.

A difficult memory task became an intuitive selection experience. Users could discover existing intelligence through language they already understood.

It was a relatively small interaction change, but an important step towards making AI capabilities accessible to more people.

Chapter 3

From one giant query to structured thinking

Year 3 · Making complex searches understandable

Discoverability was one problem. Understandability was another.

Even when users had replaced hundreds of keywords with AI entities and topics, their searches were still difficult to interpret.

A complex query was essentially a wall of text. You could build it, but could you read it back and confidently explain what it was doing? Could a teammate understand it?

We needed to change how users saw the logic behind a search.

The design shift

We decomposed the single, intimidating query string into clearly labelled rows, grouping criteria such as companies, topics and exclusions.

Instead of scanning a wall of terms, users could see the individual components and understand how they contributed to the overall search.

A search became something closer to a document: structured, readable and easier for a teammate to audit.

The UX shift

From query syntax → to visual structure.

This was perhaps the quietest but most important step in the evolution. Once the underlying logic became visible, we created the foundation for users to understand, manage and eventually take greater control of their searches.

But a new question emerged. We had reduced the number of keywords. We had made searches easier to read.

So why were users still building multiple interconnected searches?

Because making the structure visible didn’t necessarily make it flexible enough.

Chapter 4

From rigid categories to flexible grouping

Year 4 · Letting users define what belongs together

Our structured search experience made searches easier to understand, but the system still imposed an important limitation: users could only group entities of the same type together.

In reality, business questions don’t always follow those predefined categories.

Consider a user who wants to track Mars Inc. and its products across UK media:

(Mars Food OR M&M’s Chocolate OR Skittles OR Galaxy) AND Mars Inc. AND UK media list

From a business perspective, these brands and products belong together. The user wants to capture coverage mentioning any of them, within the context of Mars Inc. and UK media.

But our system treated organisations, products and other entity types as separate categories. Users couldn’t bring them together into one group and define their own OR logic across them.

The system understood entity types. Users understood business relationships. Those two ways of thinking didn’t always align.

The design shift

We introduced flexible grouping, allowing users to combine different types of entities, topics and sources within the same group, then choose how they relate using AND, OR and NOT operators.

Users could finally organise search criteria around the meaning of their business question, rather than the categories imposed by the system.

The UX shift

From system-defined categories → to user-defined groupings.

Search became more flexible, allowing users to express the relationships they actually cared about without splitting their logic across multiple searches.

But there was still a fundamental assumption underneath everything we’d built: that users came to Signal wanting to build searches.

What if that assumption was wrong?

Chapter 5

A new era: from building searches to asking questions

The next paradigm · Search 2.0 and AI-powered tagging

People don’t come to Signal because they want to build searches.

They come with questions about their business.

That’s the job they want to get done. They don’t necessarily care how many entities, keywords, operators or search groups it takes to get there.

This called for more than another improvement to the search interface. It called for a completely different UX paradigm.

What the product asked for

("AI deployment" OR "AI workflow*" OR "deploy* AI" OR "AI rollout") AND ("Acme Corp" OR Acme) AND (UK Tier 1 OR "UK nationals") NOT (job* OR hiring OR careers)

The design shift

Instead of asking users to construct increasingly sophisticated search queries, we separated the experience into two simple concepts:

1. Define your content universe. Users establish the pool of information they want to work with, using broad criteria such as entities, reading lists and sources.

2. Describe what they’re looking for. Users express their business question in natural language, creating an AI tag that classifies relevant articles across that content universe.

The AI agent handles the complexity of interpreting the definition and tagging the articles — with the process continuing as new coverage arrives.

The interface no longer asks users to understand the machinery behind search. It gives them a simple way to tell the system what matters to them.

The UX shift

From constructing queries → to expressing intent.

This is a fundamental change in the relationship between users and the product. We’re moving away from making users do the work of translating business questions into technical search logic, towards a system that can understand and act on their intent.

Chapter 6

Building confidence into the AI experience

The latest addition · Definition refinement

A new paradigm also introduces a new challenge: confidence.

Writing an AI tag in natural language sounds simple, but how can users know whether the system has understood what they mean?

Sending a definition into the system and hoping it works isn’t a particularly reassuring experience — especially when users are about to apply it across large volumes of content. We wanted to remove that uncertainty before users committed to using their tags.

The design shift

We introduced a definition refinement experience directly into the workflow.

Rather than making users leave the setup process, consult external guidance or figure out how to improve their prompts themselves, we bring the assistance to where they need it.

Users review real borderline articles and simply indicate whether each one should or shouldn’t receive the tag. The system uses these decisions to help refine the definition.

This makes the experience:

  • Less friction: Refinement happens within the existing workflow.
  • More intuitive: Users can make decisions based on real articles rather than trying to write the perfect prompt.
  • More confidence-inspiring: Users can validate and improve their definition before applying it at scale.

The goal is simple: help users get to a working AI tag they trust, without having to worry about the cost of getting it wrong across a large volume of data.

The UX shift

From hoping the AI understands → to confidently validating its understanding.

What’s next?

We’re not finished evolving search.

What started as an effort to help customers manage their own searches has grown into something much bigger: rethinking how people interact with information and how much of the complexity technology should take away.

  • keywords→tointelligence
  • rigid structures→toflexible logic
  • query building→toexpressing intent

The next chapter is still being written.

The direction remains the same: less effort spent telling the system how to work, and more time spent understanding what matters.
