Designing the author manuscript tracker

Authors submitting to the new peer review system had no way to see where their manuscript was. We researched what they needed to know, designed a tracker in plain language, and measured what changed.

01 · Background

4,000 submissions a month, and no status tracker

By mid-2020 the new peer review system was receiving more than 4,000 submissions a month. But once authors submitted, there was nothing to tell them what happened next.

02 · The problem

Frustrated authors, overwhelmed customer support

Authors were left completely in the dark, and the customer service team was handling a high volume of calls about it.

03 · Our hypothesis

Tracking would meet a basic expectation

We felt confident that by helping authors track the status of their submission, we would meet authors’ basic expectations, increase their satisfaction and reduce the business cost.

But we didn’t know exactly what authors needed or expected:

  • Which authors need updates on their submissions?
  • Why, and when?
  • At what level of detail?

04 · Design process

Brainstorming solutions as a team

We started with a team sketching session. Everyone sketched how a status tracker might work, and we marked the ideas worth taking forward.

05 · Design process

Prototype test and concept validation

We turned the sketches into a prototype to test the concept with authors: a list of submissions with their current status, and a detail view with the full history.

07 · What we learnt

Under pressure, and in the dark

Motivation and context

“I am under pressure!”

Behaviour and pain points

“Communication breaks down. I feel anxious!”

Expectation

“Tell me what happened, when, for how long, and what’s next.”

We mapped authors’ current experience against five things they need from the process. Three of the five left them frustrated.

08 · Key takeaways

What the tracker had to do

  1. 1Give authors the context of why.
  2. 2Put the latest update first.
  3. 3Tell authors what they need to do and the expected timeframe.
  4. 4Link straight to the right app so authors can complete the task.

09 · Design process

Design iteration

We iterated on the Submissions Manager across desktop, tablet and mobile: tabs for manuscripts in progress, published and rejected; the latest update first; and each pending task with its due date and a button to complete it.

10 · Plain language

Use language authors will understand

11 · First release

Live on 14 June 2020

The first release went live on 14 June 2020. In this walkthrough, an author submits a manuscript, follows the link from the confirmation page and finds each submission’s current status in plain language.

12 · The outcomes

Less frustration, far fewer tickets

Authors were less frustrated by a lack of tracking information, and support tickets went down dramatically.

We also mapped the author experience again after the first release. Four of the five stages moved up, and none moved down. Compare before and after in the journey map.

13 · Continued improvements

What we’d improve next

There were still plenty of areas to improve.

  • A coherent journey

    Let authors reach tracking information from their account.

  • An overview that eases anxiety

    A progress updates view.

  • A more transparent process

    If there’s a delay, say why.

  • A clear contact

    Show authors who to contact from the start.

Highlights

  • Team collaboration through the whole process.
  • Early, in-depth user research gave us a strong foundation for the design.
